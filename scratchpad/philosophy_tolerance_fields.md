# TOLERANCE FIELDS

## A visual philosophy

---

There is a gap between what a thing is declared to be and what a thing is. Every drawing that has
ever been made from a specification contains it. The nominal and the actual are not the same edge,
and the distance between them — small, patient, usually invisible — is where all real work happens.
**Tolerance Fields** is a movement that takes that gap as its only subject. It does not resolve the
discrepancy; it renders it, at scale, with reverence, until the viewer understands that the
disagreement *is* the form.

**Space and form.** Compositions are built from nested boundaries that refuse to coincide. A single
mass — one numeral, one letterform, one silhouette — is surrounded by two, three, four containment
lines, each claiming a different extent for the same body. None is wrong. Each was measured by a
different instrument, and each instrument was honest. The frames are drawn with the flat, unhurried
authority of an engineering elevation: hairlines that never vary, corner ticks that terminate
exactly, extension lines that stop short of touching the thing they describe. Negative space is not
emptiness but *clearance* — the declared allowance around a body, and therefore as meaningful as the
body. The work must read as though every hairline was placed by hand, checked, and placed again;
the product of deep expertise, not of arrangement.

**Colour and material.** A ground of uncoated paper — warm, slightly yellowed, with the faint
directional tooth of a laid stock — because measurement belongs to paper before it belongs to
screens. Against it, one ink: dense, matte, absolute. And one chromatic actor, used with real
discipline, which appears *only* where the actual departs from the nominal. Colour is never
decoration in this movement. Colour is the residual. When a viewer sees the accent, they are seeing
error made visible, and the restraint of that single hue is what gives it force. Fills are flat.
Shadows, where they occur, are hard, offset, unblurred — the honest shadow of a cut sheet lifted off
another sheet, never the soft glow of a rendering. Nothing is faded to make it tasteful; a mark too
timid to be seen is simply dirt on the page.

**Scale and rhythm.** One body is enormous. Everything else is small, and the smallness must feel
deliberate rather than merely reduced — annotation scale, the size of a hand-lettered note beside a
component. The jump between these two registers is violent and there is nothing between them; the
middle scale is the enemy of this movement because it dilutes both. Repetition carries the argument:
the same measurement taken again, and again, and again down a column, each result differing in the
last digit. Accumulation is the proof. A single reading is an assertion; two hundred readings
arranged in a patient grid are a discipline, and the eye reads that patience directly, without
being told.

**Composition and balance.** The page behaves like a plate from an instrument-maker's manual — a
document from a field that does not exist, but which clearly has standards, conventions, and a
century of practice behind them. Elements sit on an invisible grid that is never violated, and
alignment is absolute: if two marks relate, they share an axis exactly, and if they do not relate,
they share nothing. Rotation appears rarely and only where it means something, because in this
movement a tilted object occupies more room than its own width, and that fact is itself part of the
subject. Balance is achieved by weight, not by symmetry. Every composition should survive being
looked at for an hour, revealing another tier of order each time — this is the standard, and it is
reached only through painstaking attention and many, many passes.

**Visual hierarchy and text.** Text is an instrument reading, not a voice. It appears as figures,
units, tolerances, index marks, a specimen number in a corner — clinical, monospaced or thin, set at
annotation scale, and never explaining what the image already proves. At most one phrase in the
entire composition may speak in a human register, and it earns that by being nearly hidden. Nothing
overlaps. Nothing touches an edge it was not designed to touch. Every element has clearance,
because clearance is the subject. The finished plate must look like it was laboured over by someone
at the absolute top of their field — obsessive, unhurried, master-level — an artefact that makes an
invisible discrepancy feel like a phenomenon worth a lifetime of study.

---

*Tolerance Fields holds that precision is not the absence of error but the measurement of it. The
declared edge is a claim. The drawn edge is a fact. Art lives in the space between, and that space
must be rendered with more care than either.*
